🔵 Kick Drum

A very fast frequency sweep from ~200 Hz down to ~40 Hz, with a sharp amplitude spike. The low thump you feel in your chest at concerts. Try putting it on beats 1 and 3.

🟢 Snare

White noise mixed with a short tone burst (~180 Hz). The noise creates the "crack" texture, the tone gives it pitch. Classic placement: beats 2 and 4.

🟡 Hi-Hat & Open Hat

High-passed white noise — only the highest frequencies remain, creating the metallic "tsss" sound. Closed hat is shorter; open hat rings out. These add groove and subdivide the beat.

Step 3 — The Science

How Sound Works

Sound is vibrating air. Everything musical — every drum hit, melody, and chord — is built from these basic physical properties.

The 4 Basic Waveforms

The waveform is the shape of the vibration. Same frequency, same volume — but completely different character. Click any waveform to hear it at 220 Hz.

🔵 Sine: Single frequency, no harmonics. Pure, clinical. Tuning forks, flutes, test tones.
🟢 Square: Odd harmonics only (1, 3, 5…). Hollow, buzzy. Clarinets, 8-bit game sounds, bass synths.
🟡 Sawtooth: All harmonics (1, 2, 3…). Bright, rich, aggressive. Strings, brass, lead synths.
🟢 Triangle: Odd harmonics, falling fast. Soft, flute-like. Sits between sine and square.

Musical Notes & Their Frequencies

Each octave doubles the frequency. A4 = 440 Hz is the international tuning standard — every other note is derived from it by multiplying by the 12th root of 2 (≈1.0595) per semitone.

💡 Octave rule: Every time you go up an octave, the frequency doubles. A3 = 220 Hz → A4 = 440 Hz → A5 = 880 Hz. Your brain hears these as the "same note, just higher."

The Harmonic Series

When you play a note, you don't just hear one frequency — you hear a stack of frequencies at integer multiples called harmonics. It's this stack that makes a violin sound different from a piano playing the same pitch. Click a bar to hear that harmonic.

ADSR Envelope

Every sound has a shape over time — how it starts, grows, holds, and fades. This is the envelope. ADSR stands for Attack · Decay · Sustain · Release.

⚡ Attack: How long it takes to reach peak volume. Fast attack = punchy drums. Slow = swelling pads.
📉 Decay: How quickly it falls from peak to sustain level. Short decay + low sustain = plucky sounds.
🔒 Sustain: The volume held while the key is held down. 0% = no sustain (percussion). 100% = organ.
🌙 Release: How long the note fades after you let go. Long release = reverb-like trails.

How to Build a Beat

A beat is just patterns of sounds placed in time. Here's how every genre starts — and how to think about the sequencer above.

Lay the kick (the pulse)

The kick drum is the heartbeat. In most Western music it falls on beats 1 and 3 of a 4-beat bar. In the 16-step grid, beats land on steps 1, 5, 9, and 13. Start with kick on 1 and 9.

Sprinkle hi-hats (the groove)

Hi-hats fill in the space and define your tempo feel. Straight 8th notes (every other step) feel energetic. Every 16th (all steps) feels frantic. Try every 3 steps for a triplet swing feel.

4

Add melody with the synth pad

Once you have a beat going, try the synth pad above. The C Pentatonic scale (C, D, E, G, A) works over almost everything — you literally can't play a wrong note. Pick frequencies that feel good to you; trust your ear.

💡 Frequency tip: Bass notes (below 200 Hz) sit under the kick drum. Mid-range (200–2000 Hz) clashes with snare if overdone. High notes (2 kHz+) cut through without muddying the low end.
